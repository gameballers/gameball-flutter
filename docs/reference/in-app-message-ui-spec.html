How to read this

three sources of truth

Every visual property on this page comes from exactly one of three places, and which one it is decides who may change it. The distinction is the whole reason the page exists: a porter who treats a constant as themeable produces an SDK that looks different from the others, and a porter who hard-codes something the campaign controls produces one that ignores the dashboard.

Campaign

Arrives on the wire from the dashboard, per campaign. Every one of these is nullable, and the spec names what happens when it is null. Never hard-code a value in this class.

Constant

Fixed in the SDK and identical on all four platforms. Not exposed to the host, not exposed to the campaign. These are the numbers that make the same campaign look the same everywhere — changing one on a single platform is the drift this document exists to end.

Host theme

Falls through to the embedding app's theme when the campaign named nothing — Material ColorScheme on Flutter and Android, the equivalent on iOS. The message adopts the app it lives in rather than asserting a colour the brand never chose.

Specimens do not follow your theme

Everything inside a phone frame on this page is painted in its true rendered colours and stays fixed whether you read this in light or dark mode. Only the page around it themes. A specimen that changed with your OS setting would be useless as a colour reference.

The colour model

content.colors → painted surface

The backend sends one colors object per campaign with six keys, plus a colors object per button with three more. Nothing else in the payload carries colour. Each key maps to exactly one painted surface, and each has a defined fallback — there is no key whose absence leaves a surface undefined.

Accepted formats

One parser handles every colour key. It accepts #RRGGBB, #AARRGGBB, the same two without the hash, and a raw 32-bit integer. Six hex digits are promoted to full opacity. Anything else — wrong length, non-hex, a named colour, rgb() — logs and returns null, which means that one slot falls back while the rest of the message renders normally. A malformed colour never costs the customer the message.

Alpha is honoured everywhere. #80FF0000 is a half-transparent red, and it is accepted on the message background as readily as on the scrim. A campaign can therefore make a modal card translucent. That is deliberate and must behave identically on every platform — do not clamp alpha to opaque on the surface.

What the live backend does not send

Across all fifteen campaigns on the alpha account, closeButton, border and frame are null on every single one, and every button's colors object is null too. Two consequences a porter needs to plan for:

  • The scrim and close-glyph fallbacks are not edge cases — they are the path in production today. Get them exactly right; they are what every customer sees. The close glyph in particular is always derived, never named.
  • Every button on the live account renders as a bare text button in the host's primary colour, with no fill and no border. If the intended final look is a filled call-to-action, that is a dashboard gap, not an SDK one. Build the fill and border support regardless — the wire contract has always carried it.

The close button

three cases, no disc

The close affordance is the one control that must stay legible against a surface the SDK has never seen — a dark glyph on a dark card is not a styling flaw, it is a customer who cannot close the message. It carries no disc, ring or shadow. The contrast comes from the glyph colour itself, which is derived rather than defaulted.

Why derived rather than a fixed default

Neither half of the pair is safe on its own, and both failures are live cases rather than hypotheticals. A fixed white glyph measures 1.98:1 against the pale water on the QA poster and 1.00:1 against a white card — invisible. A fixed dark glyph measures 1.00:1 against the #111827 background the live slideup campaign uses. WCAG 2.1 asks 3:1 of a non-text control; the derived pair clears it against every background, worst case 3.8:1.

This replaces an earlier rule that keyed the colour off whether the message had artwork. That was wrong twice: a contained portrait image letterboxes, so the glyph usually sits on card background even though the message does have an image — which painted a white glyph onto a white card on three live campaigns; and naming closeButton switched the contrast treatment off, so the one field a marketer is most likely to touch was the one that could hide the control.

The one case the derivation cannot guarantee

Over full-bleed artwork — a fullscreen image_only — the message background is not what is behind the glyph, so case 2 is a reasonable guess rather than a guarantee. On the live QA poster it happens to land well (a dark glyph on pale water is 8.96:1, where the old white glyph was 1.98:1), but a dark poster with a light background colour would go the wrong way. That is the campaign that should name colors.closeButton, and it is the same case Braze leaves to the marketer. Worth a dashboard nudge on image-only campaigns specifically — not a requirement across the board.

Glyph

Material's Icons.close on Flutter and Android. iOS uses the equivalent SF Symbol (xmark) rather than importing Material's — the shapes match closely enough and the platform glyph is what a customer expects.

Hit target, not glyph size

48 × 48 around a 24 glyph, on every type — the accessibility minimum on both platforms (48dp Android, 44pt iOS). All three products agree here: Braze's view is 20dp but it expands the tappable region to 48 with a TouchDelegate, and CleverTap draws a 40 visual centred in a 48 target. Keep the glyph and the target as separate numbers.

Accessibility label

Taken from the platform's own localised close string, not a literal. On Flutter that is MaterialLocalizations.closeButtonTooltip, so it is already correct in every locale the host ships — including Arabic. A hard-coded "Close" would be the only untranslated word in the module.

Corner, not side

Positioned to the trailing corner, which mirrors under right-to-left. A glyph pinned to a hard-coded right edge sits in the wrong corner in Arabic and reads as somebody else's UI.

Outside the tap surface

On every type the close control is a sibling of the tappable message body, never a child. Closing a message must never also fire its click action or count as engagement.

What the references do

Braze draws a bare glyph in both SDKs — a grey X asset on Android, the literal character ✕ on iOS — with nothing behind it even over artwork, and leaves contrast to the marketer. CleverTap ships a solid black disc with a white ring, baked into the asset, always on, and places it outside the card in the screen's corner rather than inside. This spec takes Braze's restraint and placement with a guarantee attached.

Slideup

messageType 1 · no layout variants

A non-blocking banner at one edge. It is the only type that does not demand a decision: no scrim, no buttons, no close glyph, and the app underneath stays fully usable because the overlay occupies only the banner's own band and intercepts no hit test outside it. Everything about its geometry follows from that — a banner that grew with its content would eventually cover the screen it exists not to block.

Layout is ignored on this type. A slideup renders one composition. It has no imageUrl slot at all — artwork the layout must size itself around does not fit beside three lines of text — only iconUrl, a fixed square. Buttons in the payload are dropped with a log rather than rendered.

Dismissal — the type with no guaranteed exit

A slideup draws no close glyph and has no scrim to tap, so its only affordances are a swipe and the passage of time. A campaign that sets neither leaves a banner sitting over the host's app bar until the customer guesses at a gesture nobody told them about — and three campaigns on the live account were exactly that.

The three-line clamp

Constant

Long copy ellipsises rather than growing the banner. This is the load-bearing rule of the type. Note it is a mechanism, not a number: the existing iOS SDK bounds the container's height instead, which truncates where a line clamp grows the banner — they diverge at a large accessibility text scale, so a port must implement the clamp rather than translate the cap.

The rule, and why it is a ratio

The cap is the smaller of two bounds:

// the shape bound — the same number on every device
byShape = cardWidth / minImageRatio        // 342 / 0.55 = 621.8

// the room bound — never let artwork squeeze out the call to action
byRoom  = availableHeight - copyReserve     // 796 - 120 = 676

cap = min(byShape, byRoom)

The rule this replaced capped the image at 40% of screen height while the card's width came from screen width, so the ratio at which bars appeared slid with the device — 1.013 on a tall phone, 1.226 on a short one. The same square image was clean on one and letterboxed on the other, which nobody chose and no marketer could preview. Against a ratio the crossover is one number wherever there is room for it.

Stated honestly: it is not perfectly device-independent. Where there is room, the crossover is exactly 0.55 everywhere. On a genuinely cramped screen the reserve binds first and it rises to about 0.66 — so a very tall poster picks up small bars on an SE that it would not have on a 15. That is a far narrower window than the 1.013-to-1.226 spread it replaces, every ratio down to 3 : 4 is clean on every device, and the alternative is letting artwork push the call to action off the bottom.

Edge to edge, covering the app. No card, no margin and no scrim — the surface is opaque and nothing shows past it, so there is nothing to dim. Back gesture

A fullscreen message covering the whole app with only a small glyph is the one most likely to feel like a trap, so the system back gesture dismisses it and does not pop the host's route. A modal does the same. A slideup deliberately does not — a non-blocking banner has no claim on the back gesture, and intercepting it would break navigation for a message the customer is entitled to ignore.

Why fullscreen does not scale

A surface that grows into position looks like a botched transition at that size, and scaling moves the artwork's painted bounds — which is the thing this type is measured on. The modal scales because a card arriving from an edge would imply a direction the message does not have; 4% is enough to read as arriving.

Reduce motion

All three honour the platform's reduce-motion setting by dropping the duration to zero, not by shortening it. A customer who asked the OS for less movement gets the message immediately.

There is no exit animation

Dismissal removes the overlay entry on the spot on every type — the one exception being a slideup swipe, which animates because the gesture drives it. Both references animate out, so this is the one motion decision still open. See below.

The impression fires after the first frame

Not at insertion. Insertion only schedules a frame; nothing is on screen until it paints. Counting at insert time books a view of something the customer may never see — if the app is backgrounded in that instant, frames stop and the callback correctly never runs. The auto-dismiss timer starts on the same frame, so a configured duration measures visible time.

Against the references

Animating a message in is not something we invented — both products do it, and Braze's own documentation states the rule plainly: “slideup messages slide into the screen; full and modal messages fade in and out.” Three things differ.

Three things to settle

  • The modal's scale is ours alone. Neither reference scales anything; both fade. A 4% scale is a small, tasteful touch, but it is also the one motion property that is hard to reproduce identically across four platforms — a fade is trivially identical, a spring or a curve is not. Recommend dropping it to a fade for consistency, or keeping it as a documented, deliberate difference.
  • We are the only one with no exit. Braze reverses the entrance at the same duration; CleverTap fades out. Recommend adding a reverse of each entrance. If added, dismissal analytics must still fire on the gesture, not at the end of the animation.
  • Impression timing differs by roughly one animation. Braze logs the impression when the entrance completes; we log it at the first painted frame, when a fading message is still close to invisible. Ours is the more conservative choice if an animation is ever interrupted — the view still counts — but it does mean we count a view up to 200 ms before the customer can really see it. Worth a deliberate answer rather than an accident.

"swipe" is promoted to "both" on a fullscreen message

"swipe" means “no close glyph, dismiss by tapping outside”. A modal can honour that because it has a scrim. A fullscreen message has no scrim and no swipe gesture of its own, so the same instruction leaves only the system back gesture — which exists on Android and does not exist on iOS. Unless the campaign also set autoDismissSeconds the result is unclosable.

So the parser promotes it and logs, rather than obeying it — the same refusal it already applies to an undismissable modal. Every port must do this; a port that obeys the field literally ships a trap.

Why the layout field is overridden here, and only here

A campaign keeps its message if any of header, body or image is present; the artwork gate passes messages that have no URLs at all; and the image-only composition never references header or body. So layout: "image_only" with copy and no artwork renders as bare background plus buttons, counts an impression, and reports nothing wrong. Guard it at parse: a message declared image_only with no usable artwork should fall back to the stacked composition — the one case where overriding the declared layout is right, because the alternative is a blank screen. Both types do this. It is the only place a port may disregard content.layout.
